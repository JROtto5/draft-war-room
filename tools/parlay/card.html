The whole idea, in two numbers

Two five-leg parlays. Comparable difficulty on every individual leg — each one needs a player to beat roughly 115% of their projection. The only difference is that one is built inside a single game and the other is spread across five.

Most people build the second one because it feels more diversified. In a parlay every leg must hit, so diversification is not protection — it is the thing killing you.

Why lift grows with legs

measured across all 12 games

I simulated every game jointly and searched the full space of same-game parlays. Correlation doesn't just help — it compounds. Each added leg multiplies the naive price much faster than it cuts the true probability.

Median correlation lift for the best same-game parlay at each size. The best single six-leg stack found reached 75×. Spread the same legs across different games and every one of these drops to 1.0×.

Leg count is only half of it. The depth of each threshold matters just as much — the further into the tail you push, the more the legs depend on one shared event. Same two players, same game, five different thresholds:

How to actually use this

read before betting anything
STEP 1

The file has no odds in it

This is a DFS projection export — fantasy points, ceilings, floors, salaries. There are no sportsbook lines, spreads or totals anywhere in it. So I cannot compute edge, and anyone who tells you otherwise from this file is guessing. What I can compute is the fair line. The bet only exists where the book disagrees with it.

STEP 2

Compare, then bet — never bet blind

For each leg, look up the posted line. Bet the over only if the book's number is at or below my threshold. If Chase's fantasy-score line is posted at 21.5 and my fair number is 19.8, that leg is negative value — skip it and the parlay with it.

STEP 3

Prefer fantasy-points props

The projection is denominated in DK fantasy points, so a fantasy-score prop needs no translation at all. The receptions and yardage lines in the "what it needs" column come from a decomposition with stated assumptions (12.5 yds/rec for wideouts, 155 receiving yards per TD, and so on) — useful for sanity, worse as a bet.

STEP 4

Check actives first

Four notable players carry no projection here, which is how I identified the leverage spots — but it also means this file encodes a specific injury picture. If it has since changed, several legs change with it.

What would make me wrong

the honest failure modes

The median trap — the one that would cost you most

The fpts column is a median, not a mean. I verified this: the geometric mean of ceiling and floor lands within 1% of it across 170 players. Because the distribution is right-skewed, the mean sits meaningfully higher — and most public projections you'd compare against are means. If a book sets its line at a mean-based projection, the over is a 43% shot for a quarterback and 38% for a receiver, not a coin flip. Every "50%" on this card assumes the line is at the median. A line two points higher quietly turns a fair bet into a bad one.

Injury and game-script risk is nowhere in this model

The distribution describes a player who takes a normal, healthy workload. It assigns zero probability to an inactive scratch, a first-quarter exit, or a blowout benching. That barely matters for a longshot; it is the whole story for the Anchor. Shade every probability here down by roughly 2–4% before comparing to a real price.

The correlation numbers carry more model risk than the single legs

Correlation strengths are a standard NFL structure I specified (same-team QB→WR +0.58, opposing quarterbacks +0.22), not something measured from this slate. I verified every matrix used is mathematically valid, so the lifts aren't a numerical artifact — but if the real correlations are weaker, the lift figures shrink. The direction is solid; the magnitude is an estimate.

Defenses are excluded because the data for them is broken

I found 10 of 23 defenses with a floor above their own median — impossible for a 10th percentile — and DST ceilings run about 6× the median versus 2× for skill players. The published defensive ceiling is a max case, not a percentile. No defense appears anywhere on this card, and you shouldn't let any model hand you a defensive number from this file. One skill player fails the same way: Calvin Ridley, whose 0.51 floor on a 6.11 projection implies volatility three times the calibrated band for a receiver. He's excluded too.

Three of these projections are contingencies, not distributions

Michael Mayer's 10.8 exists only because Brock Bowers has no projection; Travis Etienne's 17.0 only because Kamara has none. That isn't a smooth distribution around a likely outcome — it's a coin already flipped. If the absent player is active, the projection isn't merely optimistic, it's void, and the whole Leverage parlay goes with it. The leverage spots carry the best potential edge on this card and the most binary failure. Confirm the inactive report before staking them, not after.

Don't bet the yardage translations in the tail

The "what it needs" column converts fantasy points into receptions and yards at the median, which is sound. Pushing that same conversion into the tail is not: my decomposition scales yardage by each player's fantasy-point volatility, and a large share of fantasy variance is touchdown variance rather than yardage variance. That inflates yardage tails — it would put Burrow at 350+ passing yards roughly 8–10 points too high. Use the stat lines as a sanity check on whether a number is reasonable, and place the bets on fantasy-points props, which need no translation at all.

These lines may not be purchasable

The Anchor's thresholds sit 20–35% below each player's median. Those rungs exist on alternate fantasy-score ladders at full sportsbooks; they do not exist on pick'em apps, where the deepest discount is around 15–20%. If your book doesn't post the rung, the leg doesn't exist — don't substitute the standard line and call it the same bet.

Real markets

team totals · game totals · spreads · anytime TD

Fantasy points aren't a market you can bet at most books. These are. The bridge is that every passing touchdown is also a receiving touchdown — so summing receiving TDs across a team's pass catchers must equal that team's passing TDs. That constraint isn't assumed, it's solved: it lands at 142 receiving yards per receiving TD for wideouts and 119 for tight ends, both inside the real NFL band, and puts the slate at 2.7 offensive TDs per team. The file is self-consistent once you count the full player pool.

Two independent reads, and they mostly agree

Team totals can be derived twice from this file, from opposite directions: forward from the offensive projections, and backward by inverting each defense's projection through DraftKings' points-allowed brackets. The two correlate at 0.723 — but the offensive side runs 1.9 points hotter per team. The numbers below split the difference, which lands the slate at 23.2 points per team against an NFL baseline near 22.5.

That gap is itself the finding: if it's all touchdowns, expected TDs from these projections run about 10% high — which is exactly the market a casual bettor is most likely to be taking overs in. Every anytime-TD price below is shaded for it.

The under angle

the most actionable thing here if you have real lines

Everything above hunts for overs. But the median-versus-mean gap points the other way, and it may be the single most useful finding on this page. Books and pick'em apps generally post fantasy-point lines at a consensus mean. These projections are medians. Because the distributions are right-skewed, a line posted at "the projection" already sits above the midpoint:

PositionMean ÷ medianP(over) at a mean-set lineFair price on the under
Quarterback1.064×43.0%−133
Running back1.103×41.3%−142
Wide receiver1.186×38.5%−163
Tight end1.189×38.4%−162

The effect is largest exactly where the market is loudest — high-variance receivers and tight ends in the big games. This is an assumption about how books set lines, not an observed price, so check one line before trusting it: if your book posts a receiver at or below the fair 50% figure in the table below, the effect isn't there and the under edge is gone.
